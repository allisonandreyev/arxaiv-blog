With the growth in AI capabilities and AI's increasing role as a research assistant, it is apparent that AI has a ever-expanding influence within academia. However, with this influence comes the risk of fully AI-generated content being misrepresented as genuine research. This post serves as a cautionary tale, highlighting the importance of critical evaluation and verification of research outputs, especially in an era where AI can produce convincing yet entirely fabricated scientific artifacts. As such, arxAIv asks, What if AI generates the entire paper? What would an entirely generated research corpus look like? While a large majority of the corpus resembles hallucinates — 31.5% of authors came from non-existent institutions — the collection nevertheless spreads across subtopics of computer vision in similar proportion to that in the field, names prominent AI research institutions, cites plausible journals and conferences, and maintains proper subsection order and visual format.

While now, it is quite simple for a human to discern between these figures and real ones, the post explores how a CLIP embedding model, PCA, and a linear discriminant can be used to classify these images mathematially with 87% accuracy.

02Corpus Background

The usual worry about AI and publishing is whether a language model contributed to drafting a paper's textual content. This corpus is about a more extremee case: if nothing was written at all.

Every page in arxAIv is an image. A single prompt — "generate a CVPR conference paper; the page should include methodology, experimentation, and scientific figures" — was run through a spread of text-to-image and multimodal systems: Stable Diffusion 3.5-Large, FLUX.1-schnell, Qwen-Image, HunyuanImage 3.0, HiDream-L1, Gemini 2.5 Flash, GPT-5.1, and Sora.

These pages were then treated as if they were real artifacts and put through various stages of analysis including: OCR, embedding, clustering, and a hand audit of every institution and citation.

03Image Embedding

To compare 269 images to each other mathematically, they need to be represented as numbers. The tool for that is an embedding: a function that maps an image to a point in a high-dimensional space, trained so that images containing similar attributes land near each other in the vector-space.

ArxAIv uses the OpenCLIP ViT-B-32 encoder, pretrained on laion2b_s34b_b79k. It divides each figure into 32×32 patches, runs them through a vision transformer, and emits 512 numbers.

ViT-B-32 is a Vision Transformer at the "base" size — about 88M parameters — reading 32-pixel patches.
  1. Resize. The shorter side goes to 224 pixels and the center 224×224 is cropped out. This is a lossy, non-optional preprocessing step, and it is the only place the figure's real resolution has any effect at all.
  2. Patchify. The square is cut into a 7×7 grid of 32-pixel tiles — 49 of them, the "32" in ViT-B-32. Each tile is flattened into 32×32×3 = 3,072 raw numbers and multiplied by one learned matrix down to a 768-number token. A learned position vector is added so the model knows which cell the tile came from.
  3. Attend. A 50th token, CLS, is prepended. Twelve transformer layers then let every token read every other token. This is the step that makes the output about broader relationships within the image rather than local texture.
  4. Project. The CLS token after the last layer — 768 numbers that have absorbed information from all 49 tiles — is multiplied by a final learned matrix down to 512.
  5. Normalize. Divide by its own length. Every figure is now a point on the unit sphere in 512 dimensions, so only direction carries information and overall magnitude is dicarded. That is why the comparison below is an angle rather than a distance.

Embedding Similarity

Two embeddings are compared by cosine similarity: the cosine of the angle between the two 512-dimensional vectors. It runs from −1 (opposite) through 0 (unrelated) to 1 (identical direction). Since magnitude is discarded, a bright figure and a dim one with the same content will still score high.

04Principal Component Analysis (PCA)

Principal component analysis finds the directions along which the corpus varies most and keeps the top few.

Concretely, PCA works in three steps. First standardize: each of the 512 coordinates is shifted to mean zero and divided by its own spread across the corpus, so no coordinate dominates the geometry just by happening to have a wider range. Then search: find the unit-length direction v₁ through 512-dimensional space along which the 269 points are most spread out, then the most spread-out direction among those perpendicular to it, v₂. Then project: a figure's position on the map below is two dot products, its standardized embedding measured against v₁ and against v₂.

So the axes have no defined units and no names; they are the two directions this particular cloud of 269 points happens to be longest along.

Capturing the Corpus

PC₁ accounts for 6.1% of the total variance in the standardized embeddings. PC₂ adds 5.5%. Together, the two axes of the map carry 11.6% of what varies across this corpus. Adding PC₃ — the depth axis in the 3D map at the top of this post — brings it to 15.7%. The remaining 84% is spread across 509 dimensions discarded during PCA.

However, a direction being long is not the same as a direction being meaningful. PCA ranks directions by spread alone, and spread has no relationship to the real/generated distinction. Had the class difference happened to lie in a low-variance direction, these 2 axes wouldn't be able to capture it.

Below is the corpus projected onto the first two of those axes — generated against real. Color is the ground truth.

generated (156) real (113)

The two populations pile up in different places, with a contested middle. To quantify that without leaning on the projection, throw the projection away and ask a local question back in the original 512 dimensions: if I stand on a figure and look at its eight nearest neighbors, how many are the same kind as me?

Across all 2,147 neighbor links, 84.9% stay within their own population. Generated figures are slightly more clannish than real ones — 87.1% of their neighbors are also generated, against 81.7% for real figures. A model trained only on image–caption pairs, given no notion that "AI-generated" is a category, sorts them anyway.

05Linear Discriminant Analysis

Neighbor similarity is a local analysis. The global version of this question is a classifier, and the simplest, robust one is linear discriminant analysis: find the single direction that pulls the two class means apart best relative to their spread, project every point onto it, cut at the midpoint.

Fitting this on just PC₁ and PC₂ — the two axes in the map above — and evaluating it leave-one-out, so every figure is scored by a line fitted without it:

Leave-one-out refits the model 269 times, each time holding back a different figure and scoring only that one.

Linear Classifier

Fisher's rule says the best direction is w = S⁻¹(μreal − μgen): take the vector between the two class means, then divide out the corpus's covariance S so that directions along which everything already varies a lot count for less. Fitted on PC₁ and PC₂ across all 269 figures, that comes out as two numbers, and the cut-point is a third:

score = 0.223 · PC₁ − 0.169 · PC₂ − 0.192

Positive means real, negative means generated.

Notice that w is not PC₁. The discriminant leans on PC₁ about four parts to three against PC₂, which is to say the direction that best separates the classes sits at an angle to the direction the corpus is merely longest along.

Validity of Result

Since 269 points in two dimensions is not many; a flexible enough procedure will find something in anything; the robustness of this experiment can be evaluated by determining if the same procedure scores highly when there is nothing to find.

A permutation test answers exactly that, and it is the cheapest safeguard in statistics because it needs no assumptions about the data at all. Shuffle the real/generated labels at random, so that by construction the two classes are interchangeabl. Refit the entire leave-one-out loop on the shuffled labels and record the accuracy. Repeating this 2,000 times creates an accurate representation of whatthis exact procedure scores on noise.

Permutation Test

Over 2,000 label shuffles, the leave-one-out accuracy averaged 50.1% with a standard deviation of 5.4 points, and the single luckiest shuffle in two thousand reached 61.7%.

The observed 87.0% is 6.8 standard deviations above that null, and not one shuffle in two thousand came within twenty-five points of it — so p < 0.0005. Resampling the 269 figures with replacement puts a 95% interval on the accuracy itself of 82.2% to 90.3%.

Note what the null is not. It is not 50% because the classes are unbalanced and the search has freedom; it lands at 50.1% here, but run the same shuffle against the best-threshold-on-image-width rule from section 11 — a rule that searches every possible cut point — and its null mean is 59.3%, nine points above chance before any real signal.

Rule Scores under Random Labels discriminant, shuffled labels width threshold, shuffled labels

As such, the separation is not noise. However, while a permutation test can tell a real effect from an imaginary one, and it cannot tell a real effect from a real confound. If the two populations differ in some way that is not related with being generated, the shuffled labels would eliminate that difference along with everything else, and the test reports a strong result either way. Significance answers correlation but never proves causation

06CLIP Ablations

Even with these findings, there's a chance CLIP isn't detecting anything semantic at all. Maybe generated figures are just blurrier, or more colorful, or more crowded, and any pixel statistic would do the same job.

So, below are seven computed appearance statistics over the same 269 images: edge density, ink coverage, colorfulness, mean saturation, tonal entropy, white space, aspect ratio; visualized separating the corpus.

Note all of these characteristics operate after the resize in step 1 of the encoder, which means none of them can see how much resampling a figure went through prior.

AUC is the chance that a randomly picked real figure scores above a randomly picked generated one, where 0.5 is a coin flip and 1.0 is perfect separation.
Separation by Pixel Statistics

Bar length is scaled from AUC 0.5 to AUC 1.0. The best of them reaches 0.589.

Real figures have marginally busier edges and a bit more color, and generated ones fill a bit more of the page with ink, but those differences are small enough to be insignificant to the variation within each group. Incorporating all 7 into a classifier together reaches 63.9% accuracy, which is six points above chance.

Leave-one-out Accuracy by Pixel Statistics

So: pixels don't explain the separation, but one thing that might hasn't been ruled out. Given that the discriminant works, what part of a figure is it most reliant on?

Image Patch Analysis

Step 2 of the encoder cut every figure into a 7×7 grid of 49 tiles. Take a figure, replace exactly one tile with flat gray, and recalculate result through the frozen pipeline — the same encoder, the same standardizer, the same two principal components, and the same three-number discriminant from section 5. Repeating this process for each of the 49 tiles in each figure creates a map of which parts of the image are most important for the classification.

The first result is about the method, not the corpus

81% of all 13,181 deltas point the same way: hiding a tile — any tile, anywhere, in either population — moves the score toward "generated," by an average of −0.20. In other words, every single map would come out a flat blue wash.

Subtract each figure's own mean delta from its 49 values, so the map no longer reports "did hiding this tile move the score" but "did hiding this tile move it more, or less, than hiding an average tile of this same figure." After centering, the signs split almost exactly evenly.

Any saliency method that perturbs an input has some version of this problem; the only way to see it is to look at the distribution of the raw effect before interpreting any single one of them.

So: a tile shaded orange is one the decision relied on more than this figure's average, in the direction of "generated." A blue tile relied toward "real." Pale tiles are ones nearly irrelevant to the classifier.

Patch Influence on Discriminant

Another important nuance is the effect of text on classification: it is possible the garbeled text or mispellings in the figures have a disprorportionate effect on the classification, rather than the graphic structure of the image itself. To evaluate this, I ran OCR over each figure at full resolution, pushing the word boxes through the same resize-and-crop geometry, covering every tile a word touches, and comparing influence on text tiles against influence on the rest.

Text Tile Weight Analysis

In the generated half, text-bearing tiles average 0.124 of centered influence against 0.106 for the rest — a gap of +0.018, 95% CI [+0.009, +0.026], and text comes out ahead in 69% of figures. In the real half the gap is +0.019, CI [+0.008, +0.032], ahead in 64% of figures.

So, text tiles carry roughly 10 to 17% more weight than non-text tiles, not several times more. As such, printed text is a contributor to the classification but nowhere near the whole of it.

Notably, OCR finds usable text in 76% of generated figures against 86% of real ones, and marks a mean of 9.0 tiles per generated figure against 15.4 per real one.

No-Text Ablation

Everything above is a statement about textual influence within an image. But it cannot answer the question underneath, which is how the discrminant would perform without text.

Blanking anything at all would affect the embedding. So the run includes a control: the same number of boxes, at the same sizes with randomized positions on the same figure. Without it, the cost of losing pixels could be misinterpreted as the cost of losing text.

Leave-one-out Accuracy With Text Removed

1/4 Text.

With every detectable word painted out, the discriminant still reaches 78.8% against a shuffled-label null of 50.2%. Measured against the majority baseline, removing the text costs about 25% of the signal.

As such, printed text is a real, measurable contributor — but it is not the mechanism.

One caveat that cannot be measured away: OCR cannot find the most degraded text, so "every detectable word" is not "every word." The masking is also slightly uneven — it blanks 4.6% of a generated figure against 5.1% of a real one.

07Pearson Correlation

Inside the generated half: CLIP identifies the two populations differ, but not how a fake figure fails. Identifying this means inventing new measurements and then asking whether they agree with each other — correlation.

The technique this section is about is Pearson correlation. Four hand-built diagnostics over the 156 generated figures are the example:

  • CLIP caption agreement — cosine similarity between the figure and the caption printed beneath it. Low means the picture and its own caption are about different things.
  • Structural complexity — density of edges and contours in the rendered panel.
  • Gibberish ratio — share of raw-OCR tokens that are not words in any language, reported with the token count it was computed over, because that denominator turns out to matter more than the ratio does.
  • Repetition — count of duplicated glyph runs, the telltale of a model that has lost its place mid-legend.

Caption-Image Agreement in CLIP

The first of those diagnostics should look impossible. Section 3 transformed an image into a 512d embedding; a caption is a sentence.

CLIP is two encoders, not one. Beside the vision transformer from section 3 sits a text transformer: the caption is tokenized, run through 12 layers, and the final token is projected down to 512d and normalized. The two encoders are trained together on a single objective. Take a batch of N image–caption pairs, compute all N×N similarities, and push the N matched pairs up while pushing the N²−N mismatched pairs down.

So caption agreement is cos(encode_image(figure), encode_text(caption)): low means the figure and the caption printed beneath it are misaligned.

Pick any two and look for structure. Hover a point to see the figure behind it. Each pairing reports a Pearson r, with a meter underneath showing where that coefficient falls against the range this corpus produces by chance.

Pearson r measures how tightly two metrics track one straight line. 0 means knowing one tells you nothing about the other; +1 and −1 are perfect straight-line agreement and disagreement. Squaring it gives r², the share of the variation in one metric the other accounts for — so r = 0.3 is r² = 0.09, or nine percent. Importantly, r sees only straight lines, so a strong curved relationship can score near zero; and with 156 points, coefficients below about |r| = 0.16 turn up routinely when there is no linear relationship at all.

Implications

Nothing above is specific to synthetic figures; this analysis can be run on any dataset with two continuous variables. In order to address 2 most common failure modes, it is important to follow these guidelines: First: compute the noise floor before reading the coefficient. At n points, |r| below roughly 2/√n can frequently occur with no relationship at all. Second: count how many pairings were used. Every panel flipped past is a test, and the chance of a spurious hit grows with the number of them; dividing tolerance by that count is the simplest available fix.

Gibberish Ratio

Run OCR over a generated figure and the median haul is 7 tokens. 38 of the 156 figures give up three tokens or fewer. On six of them OCR recovers nothing at all.

Worse, a figure whose text has dissolved completely does not score a high gibberish ratio; it scores no ratio, because OCR cannot identify the tangles as text. As such,a low gibberish ratio should be interpreted as "OCR found a few legible tokens," rather than "the text is fine."

So a figure can be structurally intricate and completely illegible. It can be perfectly legible and describe the wrong experiment. There's no single axis to measure coherence along.

08OCR

Because these pages are pictures, their text has no ground truth. It has to be OCR'd back out, which can call for its own independent analysis.

However, OCR on a rendered-then-degraded page is often damaged enough that no transcriber recovers words from the image at all. As such, an LLM mapped each raw OCR extraction to its nearest sensible reading, e.g resolving acrial imegery to aerial imagery and so on down the page.

The titles serve as the control: they come from a separate column that never went through the correction pass. That is why paper 1 is still titled "A scalable object detection framework for acrial imegery" while its own abstract, corrected, opens on "Aerial imagery."

LLM Pass Analysis

Score both versions of each page by the share of their word tokens that appear in a system dictionary. The raw OCR averages 49% — fewer than half the words it reads off these pages are words — against 76% for the corrected excerpts. Mean OCR confidence across the corpus is 74 out of 100, over an average of 428 tokens a page.

Notably, rescoring the raw text from the word "Abstract" onwards gives 48% — the gap does not shrink, it widens slightly, to 28 points. So the gap is not just in the paper's metadata, but persists throughout.

OCR Limitation

Importantly, a figure whose text has been destroyed and a figure with no text in it produce byte-identical output, and any quantity computed as a fraction of detected tokens is undefined.

Structurally, 69% of the hundred pages are complete. The rest are missing a section outright; 21 pages never render a title.

09Metadata Analysis

I checked every affiliation on all 100 pages by hand and sorted them into three kinds: hallucinated (the institution doesn't exist), mutated (a real institution wearing a wrong name — University of Adelaid, Sun Yat Tsen), and real.

Affiliations Breakdown ■ invented ■ mutated ■ real institution

The names collapse toward the mode in the same way. Across 340 invented authors, a few surnames are much more common than the rest.

Surname Frequency

"Chen" appears 28 times among 340 authors. At the level of full names, Kevin Chen shows up four times, David Chen four times, and John Doe four times.

Surname Variability

  • Distinct-per-author. 166 distinct surnames across 341 authorships, a ratio of 0.49. Half the authorships reuse a surname that has already appeared.
  • Shannon entropy. 6.63 bits against a ceiling of 8.41 — the value it would take if every authorship carried a different surname.
  • Head share and Gini. The top ten surnames cover 34.9% of all authorships and the single commonest covers 8.2%; the Gini coefficient over surname counts is 0.448.

Implications

Concentration statistics are easy to compute and easy to misuse, because every one of them is sample-size dependent — entropy in particular rises with n almost mechanically. Two corpora of different sizes cannot be compared on these numbers directly: subsample to a common size first, or compare nothing.

10Topic clusters

The technique is cluster validity: modularity, which asks whether a grouping is really there in the data, and the adjusted Rand index, which asks whether two groupings agree. Both take any graph and any partition.

Every page was read and filed under one of five computer-vision subtopics by hand. Tallied up, the hundred papers land in proportions that look like a plausible portion of the field: segmentation the largest, generation the smallest, detection and features close behind.

Separately, the hundred titles were embedded with all-MiniLM-L6-v2 — a sentence transformer — and each linked to its nearest neighbors to make the graph further down.

The 5 Clusters

Embedding-based Clustering

The five colors in the graph below are the hand-assigned subtopic labels, painted onto a graph built from title embeddings.

Modularity scores a partition by how much more edge weight falls inside groups than you would expect if the same edges were rewired at random. Zero is chance; above about 0.3 usually indicates real clustered structure. The hand labels score 0.19 on this graph. Running spectral clustering on the same 600 edges and asking it for five groups reaches 0.59 — three times better — and its five groups agree with the hand labels almost not at all, at an adjusted Rand index of 0.05, where 0 is what two unrelated partitions score.

Implications

Coloring a layout by a pre-existing label proves nothing on its own, because the layout was computed without ever seeing the label — and a force-directed graph will look structured whether or not any structure is there.

Two cheap checks separate a real finding: Modularity asks whether your grouping is actually in the data: how much more edge weight falls inside groups than random rewiring would give, where 0 is chance and above about 0.3 suggests real community structure. Adjusted Rand index asks whether two groupings agree, corrected for the agreement you would get by luck. Run your labels against what an unsupervised method finds on the same graph. If the label modularity is far below what clustering achieves, and the ARI is near zero, then what you are looking at is your own annotation, drawn on top of structure that is about something else.

11Implications and Limitations

Implications and Future Work

  • The two populations differ in resolution. The median generated figure is 483 pixels wide; the median real one is 994. One threshold on image width — call anything wider than 828 pixels real — classifies 94.8% of the corpus correctly, missing 14 of 269 — the same 94.8%, and the same fourteen-figure error budget, as the best classifier anywhere in this post. Concretely, the real figures are downsampled about 2.8× on their way into the encoder and the generated ones only 1.2×, so the real half arrives smoother and the generated half arrives with more high-frequency detail intact.
  • The diagnostics in section 7 were only ran on the generated half. They describe how synthetic figures fail. They say nothing about how often real ones would respond to the same thresholds, and several probably would.
  • This corpus is images of papers, not text. The pages were drawn, so a system emitting LaTeX or plain text is a different structure I propose as a future direction.

Future Work

  1. Control for resolution. Restriction: the two populations overlap at short sides of 246–822 pixels, which contains 92 generated and 89 real figures — refit the whole pipeline on that band alone, or better, on caliper-matched pairs within it. Destruction: keep all 269 but force every figure through one common low resolution first, so detail above it is destroyed for everyone equally. Both need a control against the same reduction in sample size or sharpness.
  2. Build the second corpus. Papers that are written rather than drawn, as argued above — the experiment that decides whether any of the findings generalize past image models.
  3. Score the section 7 diagnostics on the real half. They currently describe only how synthetic figures fail. The gibberish ratio and token count could run on the 113 real figures.
  4. Find a baseline. The surname concentration in section 9 and the subtopic proportions in section 10 are measured but not compared. A real proceedings, scored the same way and subsampled to the same size, would turn both into findings.

Data & Reproduction

Figure embeddings: OpenCLIP ViT-B-32 / laion2b_s34b_b79k, L2-normalized, standardized, reduced with PCA, joined into a k=8 cosine nearest-neighbor graph. Title embeddings: all-MiniLM-L6-v2. Classifiers are leave-one-out linear discriminant analysis. Page text is OCR followed by an LLM pass that resolves garbled runs to their nearest sensible reading, so quoted excerpts are corrected rather than raw (section 8). Affiliation and reference audits are manual. Everything the page draws lives in static/data/arxaiv.json; the notebook that produced the embeddings ships with the dataset release as tools/colab_embeddings_and_graph_data_generation.py.

The section 6 occlusion maps are tools/occlusion.py, which re-embeds the 269 figures in static/images/, refits the standardizer, the PCA and the discriminant, aligns component signs against the shipped map, and then hides each of the 49 input tiles in turn. Refitting from the images in this repository alone recovers the published coordinates at r > 0.999 on all three components, so the map can be rebuilt without the source archive. The PCA variance shares in section 4 and the image-dimension figures in section 11 are derived in tools/build_data.py.
